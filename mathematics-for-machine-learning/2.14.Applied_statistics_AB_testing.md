frequentist hypothesis testing and product A/B-test design — a staple of applied-scientist interviews
(Amazon especially) — are otherwise not covered here.*

---

## 14.1 Hypothesis Testing Fundamentals

Set up a **null hypothesis** $H_0$ (no effect / status quo, e.g. "the new checkout flow has the same
conversion rate as the old one") and an **alternative** $H_1$. Collect data, compute a **test statistic**,
and decide whether to reject $H_0$.

**Errors:**
- **Type I error ($\alpha$)**: reject $H_0$ when it's actually true (false positive) — "we shipped a
  change that we thought helped, but it didn't." Controlled by the chosen significance level (commonly
  $\alpha = 0.05$).
- **Type II error ($\beta$)**: fail to reject $H_0$ when $H_1$ is actually true (false negative) — "we
  missed a real improvement." **Power** $= 1-\beta$ is the probability of correctly detecting a real
  effect of a given size.

> **Intuition.** $\alpha$ and $\beta$ trade off against each other for a fixed sample size: making the
> rejection threshold stricter (lower $\alpha$) reduces false positives but increases false negatives
> (lower power) unless you collect more data. This is the same bias/variance-style tension seen
> elsewhere in this book — you cannot improve both simultaneously without more information (more
> samples), only shift where the error mass falls.

> **Usage.** In a product A/B test, Type I error means shipping a change (and the engineering/ops cost
> that goes with it) that provides no real benefit; Type II error means discarding a genuinely good
> change. Which error is more costly is a business decision — this asymmetry is exactly why $\alpha$ and
> the target power ($1-\beta$, typically 0.8) are chosen deliberately, not just defaulted to 0.05/0.8.

## 14.2 p-values and Confidence Intervals

The **p-value** is $P(\text{test statistic at least as extreme as observed} \mid H_0 \text{ true})$ —
*not* $P(H_0 \text{ true} \mid \text{data})$, a very common misinterpretation.

A $(1-\alpha)$ **confidence interval** for a parameter $\theta$ is an interval $[\hat\theta - z_{\alpha/2}
\cdot SE, \hat\theta + z_{\alpha/2} \cdot SE]$ such that, under repeated sampling, $(1-\alpha)$ of such
intervals would contain the true $\theta$.

> **Intuition.** The p-value asks "how surprising is this data, assuming nothing changed?" — small
> p-value means the observed effect would be unlikely under pure chance, so you reject $H_0$. It says
> nothing directly about the *size* of the effect or whether $H_0$ is "probably false" in a Bayesian
> sense (contrast with the posterior-probability framing in §8's Bayesian inference) — this
> frequentist-vs-Bayesian distinction is itself a common interview probe.

> **Practicality.** A statistically significant result (p < 0.05) with a tiny effect size (e.g., +0.01%
> conversion lift) may be **practically insignificant** — always report effect size and CI alongside the
> p-value, not the p-value alone. This "statistical vs. practical significance" distinction is one of the
> most common applied-scientist interview follow-ups.

## 14.3 Designing an A/B Test

**1. Define the metric and the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE).**
Pick a single primary metric (e.g., conversion rate) and the smallest effect size worth detecting
(e.g., a 1% relative lift) — the MDE, business context, and cost of running the test jointly determine
required sample size.

**2. Compute required sample size** (per arm), for a two-proportion test:
$$n \approx \frac{2\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2}{\delta^2}$$
where $\delta$ is the MDE (absolute difference), $\bar p$ the pooled baseline rate, $z_{\alpha/2}$ from
the significance level, $z_\beta$ from the target power.

> **Intuition.** Sample size scales as $1/\delta^2$ — detecting a smaller effect requires *quadratically*
> more data. This is why "we want to detect a 0.1% lift" is often infeasible without enormous traffic,
> while "detect a 5% lift" needs far less — a very common back-of-envelope estimation interview question.

**3. Randomize properly.** Assign units (users, sessions — pick the right **randomization unit** to
avoid interference/leakage between arms) independently and uniformly to control/treatment.

> **Intuition for why randomization matters.** Randomization is what licenses a *causal* claim
> ("treatment caused the lift") instead of a merely *correlational* one. Without it, any observed
> difference could be confounded (e.g., treatment users happened to be power users). This connects
> directly to the Bayesian-network/d-separation material in §8: randomization forcibly cuts any
> backdoor path from confounders into treatment assignment.

**4. Run for the pre-computed duration; don't peek and stop early.**
Checking significance repeatedly and stopping as soon as p < 0.05 ("peeking") inflates the true Type I
error rate far above the nominal $\alpha$ — each additional look is another chance for a false positive
under the null. Fixes: pre-register a fixed sample size/duration, or use a **sequential testing**
method (e.g., always-valid inference, mSPRT) explicitly designed to allow continuous monitoring without
inflating $\alpha$.

**5. Analyze and correct for multiple comparisons.**
Testing many metrics/segments simultaneously multiplies the chance that *some* test shows p < 0.05 by
pure chance. **Bonferroni correction** (use $\alpha/m$ for $m$ tests) is simple but conservative;
**Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR control)** is less conservative and more common in large-scale experimentation
platforms.

> **Practicality.** **CUPED** (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) is a common variance
> reduction technique: adjust the metric using a pre-experiment covariate correlated with the outcome
> (e.g., last month's spend) to shrink the metric's variance without touching $\alpha$ or the treatment
> effect estimate — directly reduces the required sample size in point 2 above. Worth mentioning by name
> in an Amazon-style experimentation-design interview.

## 14.4 Choosing the Right Test

| Scenario | Test |
|---|---|
| Compare two proportions (conversion rates) | Two-proportion z-test |
| Compare two means, known/large-sample variance | z-test |
| Compare two means, small sample / unknown variance | Welch's t-test (unequal variances) |
| Compare means across >2 groups | ANOVA (+ post-hoc correction for pairwise comparisons) |
| Non-normal / small-sample / ordinal data | Non-parametric: Mann-Whitney U, bootstrap CI |
| Sequential monitoring without inflating $\alpha$ | Always-valid inference / mSPRT |

> **Intuition.** The recurring theme: pick the test whose assumptions match the data-generating process
> (known variance vs. estimated, normal vs. skewed, two groups vs. many) — using a z-test with a small,
> non-normal sample overstates confidence, exactly analogous to using MLE point estimates (§8-9) when a
> Bayesian/regularized estimate would be more honest about small-sample uncertainty.

## Quick Interview Q&A

**Q: You ran an A/B test and got p = 0.03. Do you ship the change?**
Not on the p-value alone — check: (1) is the effect size practically meaningful given the MDE you
pre-registered, (2) was the sample size pre-computed and reached without peeking, (3) is this the
primary metric or one of many tested (multiple-comparisons risk), (4) any guardrail/secondary metrics
(e.g., latency, other engagement metrics) that regressed. Ship if all of these are consistent with a
real, meaningful, isolated effect.

**Q: How would you design an A/B test to detect a small effect on a low-traffic feature?**
Increase MDE tolerance if possible (accept detecting only larger effects), extend the test duration,
reduce variance via CUPED or a paired/pre-post design, or switch the randomization unit to increase
effective sample size (e.g., session-level instead of user-level, if interference allows) — always
weigh against increased false-positive risk from a longer window with more peeking opportunities.

**Q: What's the difference between statistical and practical significance, and why do both matter?**
Statistical significance (p < $\alpha$) only says the observed effect is unlikely under pure chance given
the sample size — it says nothing about magnitude. Practical significance is whether the effect size is
large enough to matter for the business/user given implementation and maintenance cost. A large enough
sample can make a trivially small effect statistically significant; always report effect size + CI, not
just p-value.
